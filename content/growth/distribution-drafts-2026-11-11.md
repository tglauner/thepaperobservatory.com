## Risk: one shock, two portfolios

**Narration / post:** Two portfolios can begin with the same volatility yet respond differently when one common risk mode becomes more volatile. In this synthetic example, compare a portfolio concentrated in that mode with one whose variance is spread equally across three modes. If that mode's volatility doubles, its variance quadruples: the concentrated portfolio's volatility doubles; the equally spread portfolio's rises by about 41%. This assumes fixed holdings and a fixed covariance basis. It is a scenario, not a market forecast.

## Risk: short derivative 1 — count the bets

Ten holdings are not necessarily ten independent bets. Rotate a modeled covariance matrix into its principal components and compare the share of variance contributed by each direction. The number depends on the model, the investable universe and the estimated covariance; uncorrelated components are not necessarily independent. ## Flow: the fading shock

**Narration / post:** A shock can fade while its cumulative effect remains. In the paper's stable two-variable example, later period-by-period changes shrink toward zero. Add those changes over time and the cumulative displacement approaches a nonzero value. Follow the signed links to see why. This is a reconstruction of a specified model, not an estimated market forecast or proof of causation.

## Flow: short derivative 1 — links and lags

A directed arrow in a time-series diagram tells you how one modeled variable contributes to another's next-period equation. Follow a chain of two lagged links and an effect can reach the third variable two periods later, even without a direct link. A modeled arrow alone does not establish causation. ## Flow: short derivative 2 — feedback

In a fixed linear model, feedback can make an impulse fade, persist or grow. The long-run classification depends on the largest eigenvalue modulus; boundary cases and the direction of the initial shock matter. These are model properties, not predictions of a crash.
